Hispania's only written premise: the player starts as a Celtiberian tribesman who must side with the Romans, the Carthaginians, or the Iberians. That sentence is the floor. This file is about what a Celtiberian fighter actually carried, how he fought, and how that differed from his neighbours. `[FACT - cited]` — repo README on `master`, 2020.

The best single modern English synthesis is Fernando Quesada-Sanz, *Weapons, Warriors and Battles of Ancient Iberia* (Pen & Sword, 2023). It exists specifically to kill several popular myths this file will flag. `[FACT - cited]` https://www.academia.edu/114933746/Weapons_Warriors_and_Battles_of_Ancient_Iberia_2023_

His earlier paper is the tactical core: Quesada-Sanz, "Not so different: individual fighting techniques and small unit tactics of Roman and Iberian armies within the framework of warfare in the Hellenistic Age," in *L'hellénisation en Méditerranée occidentale...* (2005/2006). `[FACT - cited]` https://www.researchgate.net/publication/238749568_Not_so_different_individual_fighting_techniques_and_battle_tactics_of_Roman_and_Iberian_armies_within_the_framework_of_warfare_in_the_Hellenistic_Age

## 1. Do not start from the guerrilla cartoon

The popular picture — Iberians as perpetual guerrillas who never formed a line — is a historiographical leftover. Quesada's whole project is to break it. `[INFERENCE]`

Livy and Polybius describe Hispanic troops exchanging throwing weapons and then drawing swords in the same sequence as Roman legionaries. Examples:

- 207 BCE: Celtiberian infantry with oval shields, backed by light troops, in a Carthaginian army under Hanno, first exchange missiles with Silanus then fight with swords. Livy 28.2. `[FACT - cited]`
- 195 BCE, Empúries: Iberians against Cato. "When the javelins and incendiary spears had been thrown, however, and the combatants drew their swords, the battle was virtually renewed." Livy 34.14.11. `[FACT - cited]`
- Livy 23.12: differences between Romans and Iberians in a given fight are framed as moral, not as a different *genus militum*. `[FACT - cited]` Quesada 2006 discussion of the passage.

Quesada's working claim: 4th–3rd century Iberian and Celtiberian infantry are dual-purpose troops, able to fight in open order and in close order, functionally close to Hellenistic *thureophoroi* and closer to a mid-Republican legionary than to a sarissa phalangite. `[FACT - cited]` abstract of "Not so different."

That does **not** mean Lusitanians and Cantabrians fought the same way. Regional difference is real. It means "Iberian = guerrilla" is a flattening, not a design primitive. `[INFERENCE]`

`[GAP FLAGGED]` Most battle descriptions are Roman, written after the fact, often moralising. Archaeology (grave panoplies, Baecula battlefield finds) is the check on the prose. Do not treat a Livy set-piece as a unit card.

## 2. Peoples the player can actually stand among

"Celtiberian" is not a single tribe. Classical writers treat it as a cluster on the eastern Meseta, between the upper Tagus and the Ebro. Core named groups in the historic period: Arevaci, Belli, Titti (Titii), Lusones. Pelendones are often adjacent; Vaccaei, Carpetani, Olcades sit on the edge of the label depending on the author. `[FACT - cited]` Britannica, "Celtiberian" and "Arevaci," https://www.britannica.com/topic/Celtiberian and https://www.britannica.com/topic/Arevaci

- **Arevaci** — usually described as the strongest. Numantia and Uxama sit in their world. Dominant partner in the mid-3rd-century confederacy that also includes Lusones, Belli, Titii. `[FACT - cited]` same.
- **Belli and Titti** — Jalón valley. `[FACT - cited]` Britannica Arevaci article.
- **Lusones** — upper Tajuña / sources of Tagus and Ebro. `[FACT - cited]` same.
- **Iberians** in the README's third option means the non-Indo-European coastal and southern communities (Edetani, Contestani, Bastetani, Ilergetes, etc.), not "anyone in Spain." `[INFERENCE]`
- **Lusitanians** are west of this cluster. Famous later under Viriathus. Not Celtiberians. `[FACT - cited]` Wikipedia summary of the distinction, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warfare_in_the_ancient_Iberian_Peninsula
- **Cantabrians, Astures, Gallaeci** — north and northwest; later, and tactically more irregular in the sources. `[FACT - cited]` same.

Second Punic War posture of the Celtiberian confederacy is often described as officially neutral, with mercenaries serving both Rome and Carthage. `[FACT - cited]` Arevaci article citing that pattern. That is exactly the fork the README already named. `[INFERENCE]`

Timeline anchors the player can touch without inventing a new war:

| Year (BCE) | What happens |
|---|---|
| 218 | Rome lands in NE Iberia. Second Punic War opens in Spain. |
| 209 | Scipio takes New Carthage. |
| 206 | Baecula; Ilipa. Carthage loses the peninsula. |
| 195 | Cato in NE Spain. Empúries pitched battle. |
| 181–179 | First Celtiberian War. |
| 155–139 | Lusitanian War; Viriathus. |
| 153–133 | Celtiberian Wars; Numantia destroyed 133. |
| 29–19 | Cantabrian Wars. Late for a "starts as a tribesman in the Punic generation" run. |

`[INFERENCE]` The README's three-way choice is sharpest in 218–206, when Carthage still fields Hispanic troops in Spain and Italy, Rome is a landing power rather than the occupying state, and coastal Iberians (Indibilis and Mandonius of the Ilergetes) are independent actors who switch. After Ilipa the Carthaginian option becomes exile, mercenary service, or memory.

## 3. What a fighter carried

Quesada's 4th–3rd century baseline panoply, Iberian and Celtiberian: `[FACT - cited]` "Not so different" abstract and the 2023 book blurb / later paper "Adapting to New Times" (UAM repository extract), https://repositorio.uam.es/bitstreams/ff0073d2-d5ec-4dd7-983e-e08156e2d5c1/download

1. A heavy throwing weapon: *soliferreum* (all-iron javelin), *falarica* (heavy socketed javelin, sometimes incendiary), or a heavy throwing spear.
2. A thrusting spear.
3. A short cut-and-thrust sword: *falcata* on the coast; antennae / La Tène-derived straight sword in the interior.
4. A round wooden shield about two feet across (*caetra*).
5. Leather helmet; sometimes felt or leather cuirass. Metal helmets and mail are elite or later.

Late 3rd century additions, especially among men serving Hannibal or Scipio: oval *scutum* / *thureos*, Montefortino ("jockey-cap") bronze helmet, longer cut-and-thrust swords. `[FACT - cited]` same.

That late-3rd-century kit is functionally close to a mid-Republican legionary (minus the old *triarii* spear line). `[FACT - cited]` Quesada, "Not so different."

### 3.1 Shields and the two infantry labels

- **Caetra**: small round buckler, often given as ~30–90 cm in secondary summaries, ~two feet in Quesada. Boss can punch. Hung when not in use. User: *caetratus*. `[FACT - cited]` https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caetrati — treat the Wikipedia equipment section as secondary; it is flagged unsourced in places.
- **Scutum / oval shield**: larger, Gallic / Hellenistic *thureos* family. User: *scutatus*. Heavier line infantry, not a different species of man. `[INFERENCE]`

Do not invent a Total-War caste system where *caetrati* can never hold a line. The sources describe the same men throwing, then drawing swords. `[INFERENCE]`

### 3.2 Swords — kill three myths

**Falcata.** Curved, single-edged cutting sword with a heavy forward blade, common in southern and eastern Iberian graves and votives. Not the universal "Iberian sword." Not a kopis clone in origin even if it looks like one. Quesada treats "the machaira/kopis/falcata problem" as a live research question, not a solved identity. `[FACT - cited]` 2023 book abstract.

**Straight Celtiberian sword.** Double-edged, good iron, pointed, usable cut and thrust. Diodorus 5.33 is the famous passage: they bury iron plates until rust eats the weak metal, then forge what remains; the sword cuts through shield, helmet, bone. `[FACT - cited]` Diodorus 5.33; widely quoted, e.g. https://www.twcenter.net/threads/units-the-iberians.731524/

Treat Diodorus's rust-refinement story as a classical marvel-anecdote sitting on top of a real fact (excellent Hispanic iron). `[INFERENCE]`

**Gladius hispaniensis.** Polybius and Livy say the Romans adopted a Hispanic cut-and-thrust sword around the Second Punic War / ~200 BCE. Archaeology of Republican *gladii* (60–70 cm parallel-edged tanged blades, wood/leather sheath with metal frame and rings) matches a Hispanic La Tène-derived type that lasted longer in Iberia than the Gallic long-sword fashion. `[FACT - cited]` Polybius 6.23.6; Livy 31.34; Quesada's sword papers summarized in the UAM extract above.

The imperial Mainz/Pompeii short swords are later. Do not dress a 218 BCE Roman in a Pompeii gladius. `[FACT - cited]` same.

**Pugio.** Hispanic dagger types are a plausible prototype for the Roman *pugio*. Literary sources do not narrate the adoption as cleanly as they do the sword. `[FACT - cited]` Quesada on metal-type adoption; flagged as a weaker literary case than the *gladius*.

### 3.3 Thrown iron

- **Soliferreum / soliferrum**: one-piece iron javelin, typically described around 1.5–2 m, slim, thickened for the grip, armour-piercing at short range because the whole shaft is dense. `[FACT - cited]` secondary synthesis at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caetrati and Quesada's weapon chapters.
- **Falarica**: heavy javelin with a long iron head; Livy describes incendiary use (pitch/tow on the shaft). Livy 21.8 (Saguntum) is the set-piece. `[FACT - cited]`
- **Amentum**: leather throwing thong. Visible on Celtiberian painted pottery (duel scenes with javelins stuck in the ground behind the fighters). `[FACT - cited]` Quesada 2023 description quoted in trade listings, e.g. https://www.everand.com/book/915687916/Weapons-Warriors-Battles-of-Ancient-Iberia

Slingers matter. Balearic slingers are the famous specialists (childhood training, heavy shot). Other Hispanic peoples used slings with smaller stone or lead bullets. Bows are rare in the warrior kit; hunting and eastern-coast influence, not a line weapon. `[FACT - cited]` Wikipedia warfare page, with the usual caution.

### 3.4 Defence

Most men: tunic, *caetra*, maybe a leather cap. Elite / later: linen cuirass, disc *cardiophylax*, greaves, bronze helmet (Montefortino becomes common in the Punic generation), occasional mail. Strabo 3.3.6 on some northwestern kits (small concave round shield, linen cuirass, leather helmet, dagger, small spears) is a snapshot of one region, not a peninsula uniform. `[FACT - cited]` Strabo 3.3.6.

Hair greaves on Celtiberians: Diodorus 5.33. `[FACT - cited]`

## 4. How they actually fought

Individual and small-unit level, Quesada's pattern:

1. Missile exchange (soliferrea, falaricae, javelins, sometimes slings).
2. Draw swords. Fight in a formation with enough frontage per man to use a throwing weapon and then a sword — not a pike-hedge.
3. Cavalry on the flanks or as a mounted-dismounted reserve.

`[FACT - cited]` "Not so different"; Livy 28.2 and 34.14.

That is why Roman adoption of Hispanic swords and (more messily) dagger types is tactically cheap: the legion already fights in that grammar. A sarissa would have forced the legion to become a different army. A *gladius hispaniensis* does not. `[FACT - cited]` Quesada's adoption paper, archived discussion at https://web.archive.org/web/20131004215345if_/http:/www.uam.es/proyectosinv/equus/warmas/online/Quesada%20Sautuola.pdf

Regional tactical colour, which should not overwrite the baseline:

- **Lusitanians**: sources stress ambush, feigned retreat, mountain fighting, Viriathus as the "barbarian Hannibal." Appian *Iberike* is the narrative spine. `[FACT - cited]`
- **Cantabrians**: *circulus cantabricus* and *cantabricus impetus* are later Roman-named manoeuvres associated with them. `[FACT - cited]` Wikipedia warfare page.
- **Champion combat**: Celtiberian painted vases show duels with swords, javelins planted behind. Classical writers mention single combat. This is real social practice, not the army's only mode. `[FACT - cited]` Quesada 2023 vase description; Almagro-Gorbea / Lorrio line of work on Celtiberian war society, e.g. https://www.researchgate.net/publication/29726494_War_and_Society_in_the_Celtiberian_World
- **Cuneus**: Arevaci associated with a wedge in some modern summaries. Treat as possible, not a unique Celtiberian super-formation. `[SPECULATION]`

Cavalry: Meseta peoples develop a serious horse arm from the 4th century. Sources praise Hispanic horse. Ilergete and Celtiberian horsemen dismount to fight when needed and keep the horse as the way out. Livy credits Hispanic cavalry at Trebia and as part of Hannibal's Italian army. `[FACT - cited]` Wikipedia warfare page; Livy on Trebia / Cannae composition.

Cannae: Polybius and Livy put Gauls and Spaniards in Hannibal's centre; Hispanic and Gallic horse on a wing. This is mercenary / allied service, not "the Celtiberian confederacy went to Italy." `[FACT - cited]` Polybius 3.113–114; Daly, *Cannae* (2005).

## 5. The social machine behind the fighter

War in Iron Age Hispania is not a territorial state system. Raiding, prestige, clientage, and mercenary wages sit next to the rare coalition army. `[INFERENCE]`

Three institutions later writers keep naming among Celtiberians and neighbouring Indo-European groups:

- **Hospitium** — hospitality / guest-friendship. Bronze *tesserae hospitales* survive (on the order of fifty known pieces in the literature). Diodorus 5.34: Celtiberians compete to host strangers. `[FACT - cited]` Ramírez Sánchez, "Clientela, hospitium y devotio," https://www.researchgate.net/publication/267526548_Clientela_hospitium_y_devotio
- **Clientela** — dependent following around a chief.
- **Devotio** — a warrior binds himself to a leader unto death. Literary attestations among Iberians and Celtiberians; the exact ritual shape is thinner than the word. `[FACT - cited]` same paper. Famous later colour: men who should not outlive the chief.

`[GAP FLAGGED]` Do not build a CK-style dynasty off *devotio*. It is a personal bond, closer to a comitatus oath than to primogeniture.

Mercenary service is a structural career, not a flavour event. From the 5th century BCE Hispanic fighters appear in Sicily, Carthage, and elsewhere, usually in small friend-and-kin contingents under their own chiefs. Strabo and the Punic-War historians treat them as high-value. `[FACT - cited]` Wikipedia warfare page; Quesada 2023 chapter list (mercenaries get their own chapter).

That is the clean historical door into the README's "side with Rome / Carthage / Iberians." The young man leaves home with a handful of kin, not as a state conscript. `[INFERENCE]`

Siege ethics in the sources are extreme on both sides: Saguntum, Numantia, Calagurris. Mass suicide, starvation, refusal to surrender. Use as tone, not as a mandatory set-piece for every run. `[FACT - cited]` Appian *Iberike* on Numantia; Livy on Saguntum.

Arevaci warrior dead exposed to vultures: Silius Italicus; stelae and Numantine pottery. `[FACT - cited]` Arevaci secondary summaries citing Silius and Aelian.

## 6. What a Hispania soldier-system should not do, on present evidence

These are findings constraints, not design orders.

- Do not make the falcata the only Hispanic sword. Interior Celtiberians are a straight-sword people. `[FACT - cited]`
- Do not make "guerrilla" the racial tactic of everyone west of the Pyrenees. Pitched battle in *instructa acies* is attested. Guerrilla is a Lusitanian/Cantabrian *and* situational tool. `[FACT - cited]`
- Do not treat Roman and Celtiberian infantry as opposite species. Quesada's point is the opposite, and it is the reason Rome can absorb Hispanic weapons. `[FACT - cited]`
- Do not start the Roman kit in AD 50. Mid-Republican panoply is the peer of the Punic-generation Hispanic kit. `[FACT - cited]`
- Do not collapse "Iberian," "Celtiberian," and "Lusitanian" into one roster row. The README already splits Celtiberian player origin from an Iberian faction option. Keep that split. `[INFERENCE]`
- Do not invent a peninsula-wide command hierarchy. Coalitions, *oppida*, chiefs, mercenary bands. `[INFERENCE]`
